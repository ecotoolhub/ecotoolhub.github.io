What Does AQI 150 Actually Mean? Decode Your Air Quality Number in Minutes

Published by Eco Tool Hub Editorial Team • October 18, 2026 • 6 min read

🌬️ Quick Answer

The Air Quality Index runs from 0 to 500. Under 50: breathe easy. 51–100: fine for most people. 101–150: sensitive groups (kids, elderly, asthma) start feeling it. 151+: unhealthy for everyone — cut back outdoors and mask up. 201+: stay inside. Behind every number is PM2.5 — at AQI 150 you're breathing about 55 micrograms of fine particles per cubic meter, roughly 4–5× the "good" ceiling.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • 100 is the yellow line: below it, most people are fine; above it, health guidance kicks in for sensitive groups first.
  • 150 is the red line: at AQI 151+, everyone — not just sensitive groups — should reduce prolonged outdoor exertion.
  • PM2.5 drives the number: particles ~30× thinner than a human hair, small enough to slip deep into your lungs.
  • Your move at each level is simple: mask (N95/KN95) at 150+, windows shut at 150+, purifier on at 200+.

The AQI Scale, Decoded

Every weather app shows the number, almost none explain it. Here is the full US EPA scale — the same one most global apps are built on — with the PM2.5 concentration hiding behind each band:

Two things jump out. First, the scale is not linear in health terms — the jump from 100 to 150 matters far more than 0 to 50. Second, AQI 150 sits at about 55 µg/m³ of PM2.5: the air literally carries four to five times the particle load of a "good" day. That's what your lungs are negotiating.

What Each Number Does to Your Lungs

PM2.5 particles are about 30 times thinner than a human hair — small enough to bypass your nose and throat and lodge deep in the lungs, and the smallest fraction can even enter the bloodstream. That's why the health effects scale with the number:

  • Under 100: for healthy adults, essentially no effect. People with severe asthma may notice symptoms creeping in near the top of this range.
  • 101–150: sensitive groups — children (their lungs are still developing), older adults, and anyone with asthma, COPD, or heart disease — start experiencing coughing, shortness of breath, and aggravated conditions.
  • 151–200: now it's everyone's problem: throat and eye irritation, coughing, and chest tightness during exercise. This is the band where a normal jog becomes a bad idea.
  • 201+: significant aggravation of heart and lung disease across the whole population. At 300+, it's an emergency: everyone stays in.

The cruel twist: indoor air can be 2–5× more polluted than outdoor air (cooking, candles, cleaning sprays all emit particles). So a bad outdoor day is the worst possible day to deep-fry dinner without the range hood on — more on that below.

How That Single Number Is Actually Calculated

The AQI isn't a direct measurement — it's a translation. Each regulated pollutant (PM2.5, PM10, ground-level ozone, nitrogen dioxide, sulfur dioxide, and carbon monoxide) gets its own sub-index from its measured concentration, using the breakpoint tables above. The highest sub-index wins and becomes the reported AQI.

In practice, PM2.5 is the driver most of the time — wildfire smoke, traffic, construction dust, and winter heating all show up as fine particles. Ozone takes over on hot, sunny afternoons in traffic-heavy cities, which is why the number can spike mid-afternoon even on a clear day. 5 Quick Wins for Bad-Air Days

Ranked by impact-per-effort, all matched to the calculator's own advice logic:

  • Keep one real N95 per person: a single well-fitted N95 beats a box of cloth masks. Replace it when breathing gets hard or the shape collapses.
  • Build a DIY box-fan purifier: a 20-inch box fan with a MERV-13 furnace filter taped to the intake — under $40, and it visibly cuts indoor particle levels in one room within an hour.
  • Seal and time your ventilation: windows shut and towels under door gaps on 150+ days; then deliberately air the whole house out when the AQI drops under 50. Ventilation is a tool — aim it.
  • Move workouts, don't cancel them: exercise indoors on red days, or shift outdoor runs to early morning before ozone and traffic peak in the afternoon.
  • Don't add to the load indoors: on bad-air days, skip the candles and incense, run the range hood every time you cook, and vacuum with a HEPA-filter machine. Your indoor air is the air you actually breathe most.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good AQI number?

An AQI of 0 to 50 is rated Good — breathe easy. Up to 100 (Moderate) is acceptable for most people. Above 100, sensitive groups should start taking it easy, and above 150 everyone should cut back on outdoor exertion.

At what AQI should I wear a mask outdoors?

At AQI 151 and above (Unhealthy, the red zone), a well-fitted N95 or KN95 mask makes a real difference outdoors. Sensitive groups — children, the elderly, and people with asthma or heart and lung conditions — should consider masking from AQI 101 upward. Cloth and surgical masks do not filter PM2.5 effectively.

Is an AQI of 100 bad?

AQI 100 is the top of the Moderate range — acceptable for most people, not an emergency. Think of it as a yellow traffic light: fine to carry on, but unusually sensitive people may notice irritation, and it's the line where air quality starts becoming a health consideration.

How is AQI calculated?

Each regulated pollutant — PM2.5, PM10, ozone, nitrogen dioxide, sulfur dioxide, and carbon monoxide — gets its own sub-index from its measured concentration. The highest sub-index becomes the reported AQI. In most cities, fine particle pollution (PM2.5) is the pollutant driving the number.
