Emergency Supplies for Babies & Small Children: A Calm Parent's Checklist

Published by Eco Tool Hub Editorial Team • November 20, 2026 • 7 min read

Quick Answer

For each baby, keep ready: about 10 diapers a day (8 for an older baby) in the next size up, roughly 20 wipes a day, rash cream, formula plus sealed water — or ready-to-feed bottles — if you formula-feed, 3 baby food pouches a day, age-correct fever medicine, two changes of warm clothes, and one familiar blanket or toy. Babies cannot ration, wait, or understand — so in any emergency, parents prepare for them first. A 7-day baby supply fits in one medium box.

Key Takeaways

  • Diapers, wipes and formula are the first shelves to empty in any disruption — stock them first.
  • Buy diapers one size up: a stored size your baby outgrows is money wasted.
  • If you breastfeed, the key supply is food and water for the mother.
  • A familiar blanket or toy is not a luxury — a calm baby helps the whole family cope.
  • Build the box gradually: one extra pack per weekly shop gets most families there in a month.

Why babies come first in every plan

Adults can skip a meal, sleep cold, and improvise. A baby can do none of those things. When shops close or shelves empty — in a blackout, a storm, any disruption — the products that vanish first are always the baby ones, because every parent in the area is looking for the same three things at the same time: diapers, wipes and formula. The parents who stay calm are the ones who already have a box at home. This is not fear; it is the same logic as keeping a spare phone charger, applied to the person who depends on you for everything.

The good news: a baby's needs are small, predictable and cheap to cover when you buy a little at a time. Everything below fits in one medium storage box and, rotated properly, costs almost nothing extra over a normal month.

The numbers: diapers, wipes, food

Plan on 10 diapers a day for a newborn, about 8 a day for an older baby. For 7 days that is roughly 60–70 diapers per baby — one large pack. Wipes run at about 20 a day once you count changes, hands, faces and the hundred small messes of a day without normal laundry; they double as the family's emergency hygiene supply too.

For food: if you formula-feed, keep one unopened tin beyond the one in use, plus sealed water reserved for mixing, and consider a few ready-to-feed bottles for the first day or two — no measuring, no boiling, no stress at 3 a.m. by torchlight. If you breastfeed, your baby's supply chain is you: the stored food and water for the mother is the baby supply. For weaning babies, plan 3 food pouches a day — they need no fridge and no cooking — plus their usual snacks.

Comfort is a supply too

Here is the part the official lists leave out, and every parent knows: a frightened baby in a dark, strange-feeling house will not settle, and a baby who will not settle means nobody sleeps, nobody thinks clearly, and a hard night becomes a miserable one. Pack the things that smell and feel like normal life — the familiar blanket, the sleep toy, two pacifiers in a sealed bag, the songs on a phone that still has battery because you were careful with it.

The same goes for toddlers: a small torch of their own turns "scary dark" into an adventure, and their favourite snacks buy patience no lecture can. Emergencies are survived by families, and families run on calm children.

The medical corner

Keep age-correct fever and pain medicine (check the dose for your child's current weight with your pharmacist, and write it on the box), oral rehydration salts — the single most important item if a small child gets a stomach bug when help is far away — a thermometer, rash cream, and any personal items like an inhaler or allergy medicine, with expiry dates checked twice a year. If your child takes regular prescription medicine, keep a buffer of at least a week beyond the current supply and rotate it so nothing expires unused.

Baby essentials, bought before you need them

Diapers in the next size up, wipes, formula dispensers and ready-to-feed supplies are cheapest on ordinary days, not during a shortage — and they are discounted often. One extra pack per shop is all it takes.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many diapers should I stock for one week?

About 60–70 per baby: 10 a day for a newborn, 8 a day for an older baby, times 7 days. Buy the next size up from what your baby wears today so the stock still fits when you need it.

What if I breastfeed?

Then the most important baby supply is food and water for you. Keep your own 3–7 day supply solid, add a manual pump if you use one, and store a small number of ready-to-feed bottles only if you want a backup for a night when you are ill or separated.

How do I keep stored baby food safe?

Pouches and jars live happily at room temperature until opened; check dates when you rotate stock into everyday use — oldest first, roughly every couple of months. Formula tins stay sealed until needed, and mixed formula follows the same rules as always: use promptly, never keep leftovers.

What belongs in a baby grab-and-go bag?

A day of diapers and wipes, two changes of clothes, one ready feed or pouches, a blanket, a sealed pacifier, fever medicine, and a written note of doses, allergies and your doctor's number — in case someone else has to care for your child.

One box, bought gradually

You do not need to buy any of this today, and you certainly do not need to buy it all at once. Add one pack of next-size diapers to this week's shop, wipes next week, pouches the week after. Inside a month, the box exists — and with it, the quiet knowledge that whatever the week brings, the smallest person in your home is covered.
