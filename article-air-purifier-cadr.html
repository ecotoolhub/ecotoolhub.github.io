Do Air Purifiers Actually Work? CADR, Room Size, and the Honest Math

Published by Eco Tool Hub Editorial Team • November 1, 2026 • 6 min read

🌬️ Quick Answer

Yes — for particles. A True HEPA purifier, correctly sized to the room, captures 99.97% of airborne particles at 0.3 microns: dust, pollen, pet dander, mold spores, and wildfire smoke. But sizing is everything — an undersized unit on low speed is an expensive white-noise machine. They do nothing for CO2, and little for gases and odors unless they include a real activated-carbon filter. The one number that matters is CADR.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • True HEPA = 99.97% capture of particles at 0.3 microns — the physics is settled, and it works.
  • CADR is the only spec that matters: your purifier's CADR (in CFM) should be at least 2/3 of your room's square footage.
  • The #1 reason purifiers "don't work" is an undersized unit running on low in a big room.
  • HEPA doesn't touch CO2 or most gases — only ventilation fixes CO2; only activated carbon helps with VOCs and odors.
  • A clogged filter silently kills performance — replace every 6–12 months or the effective CADR collapses.

What an Air Purifier Actually Removes (and What It Doesn't)

The confusion starts because "air purifier" covers several completely different technologies. A True HEPA filter is a dense mat of randomly arranged fibers that traps particles through interception, impaction, and diffusion — it is genuinely excellent at its job, and independent lab tests confirm the 99.97%-at-0.3-microns standard. What it catches: dust, pollen, pet dander, mold spores, smoke particles, and the droplets and aerosols that carry viruses.

What it does not catch: gases. Carbon dioxide passes straight through. Most volatile organic compounds (paint fumes, cleaning-product smells, formaldehyde from furniture) pass through too — those need an activated-carbon filter, and a thick one, not the thin carbon pre-filter sheet many units include as a token gesture. Meanwhile ionizers and "ionic" purifiers charge particles so they settle on surfaces (or your lungs) — and some generate ozone, itself a lung irritant. If you see an "ionic" or "ozone" feature, leave it off. For the full picture of what's floating in your home air, start with our indoor air quality guide.

The CADR Math: Size It in 60 Seconds

CADR — Clean Air Delivery Rate — is the volume of filtered air a purifier delivers per minute, measured in CFM (cubic feet per minute) in the US or m³/h elsewhere. It is tested independently by AHAM, so unlike marketing claims about "covers up to 1,000 sq ft," it is a number you can trust. The industry rule of thumb:

CADR (CFM) ≥ ⅔ × room area (sq ft). A 300 sq ft bedroom needs at least 200 CADR; a 450 sq ft living room needs at least 300. And "at least" is doing real work here — oversizing is the only safe direction, because a bigger unit cleans the same air on a lower, quieter fan speed.

Room Size Minimum CADR (CFM) What That Means in Practice
150 sq ft (small bedroom)100Most compact purifiers qualify
300 sq ft (master bedroom)200Mid-size unit; check the smoke CADR, not just dust
450 sq ft (living room)300Large unit or two medium ones
600 sq ft (open plan)400Flagship-size unit; budget models rarely reach this

One more useful formula: air changes per hour (ACH) = CADR (CFM) × 60 ÷ room volume (cu ft). Allergy organizations generally want 4–5 ACH in the breathing zone — meaning the unit filters the room's entire air volume four to five times an hour. If your purifier's marketing claims a huge room size but the CADR math gives you 1.5 ACH, the marketing is lying and the math isn't.

5 Mistakes That Make Purifiers Useless

Ranked by how often we see them — fix these before buying a second unit:

  • 1. Buying too small. The classic failure. A 100-CADR desktop unit in a 400 sq ft living room is decoration. Do the ⅔ math above before you shop, not after.
  • 2. Running it on low, always. CADR is measured at top speed. Running permanently on whisper-quiet low can cut effective cleaning by half or more. Better: oversize the unit so "medium" is quiet enough to live with.
  • 3. Never replacing the filter. A loaded filter chokes airflow, and airflow is what CADR is made of. In dusty homes or 24/7 operation, check at 6 months. The "filter light" is a timer, not a sensor — trust your eyes and the calendar.
  • 4. Hiding it in a corner. Purifiers need breathing room — at least 30–50 cm clearance on the intake sides, away from curtains and furniture. Tucked behind the sofa, it recirculates the same pocket of air.
  • 5. Expecting it to fix everything. High CO2, cooking-gas smells with no carbon filter, humidity, radon — none of these yield to HEPA. Match the tool to the pollutant: our AQI number guide helps you identify which pollutant you're actually fighting.

HEPA vs Ionizer vs UV: Which Tech to Actually Buy

Keep it simple. True HEPA + substantial activated carbon is the proven combination — mechanical filtration with no byproducts. "HEPA-type" or "HEPA-like" are unregulated marketing terms; insist on "True HEPA" or "H13." UV-C lights inside purifiers sound scientific, but the air moves past the lamp too fast for meaningful germicidal effect in most consumer units — harmless, but don't pay extra for it. Ionizers we covered: settling particles onto your furniture instead of trapping them, with possible ozone as a side effect. And ozone generators marketed as purifiers are actively harmful — ozone damages lungs at concentrations these devices produce. The boring answer wins: a big-enough box with a real HEPA filter.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do air purifiers really help with allergies?

Yes — for airborne allergens like pollen, dust mites, and pet dander, a True HEPA purifier correctly sized to the room measurably reduces symptoms in most studies. The catch is sizing: an undersized unit running on low in a large room does almost nothing. Use the CADR 2/3 rule — the CADR rating in CFM should be at least two-thirds of your room's square footage.

What CADR do I need for my room?

Take your room's area in square feet and multiply by two-thirds — that is your minimum CADR in CFM. A 300 sq ft bedroom needs at least 200 CADR; a 450 sq ft living room needs at least 300 CADR. When in doubt, go bigger: oversizing only means quieter operation, since a larger unit cleans the same air on a lower fan speed.

Do air purifiers remove viruses like COVID?

True HEPA filters capture airborne virus-carrying droplets and aerosols — the particles they ride on are well within HEPA's range. Purifiers reduce airborne concentration and therefore transmission risk, but they are a supplement to ventilation, not a replacement. No purifier makes a crowded unventilated room safe on its own.

Do air purifiers lower CO2?

No. HEPA and carbon filters do not capture carbon dioxide — only ventilation (fresh outdoor air) or dedicated CO2 scrubbers reduce CO2. If your CO2 monitor reads high, open a window or run an HRV/ERV; a purifier will happily clean the particles while the CO2 stays exactly where it was.

How often should I replace the HEPA filter?

Most manufacturers recommend every 6 to 12 months, but it depends on air quality and runtime. In a dusty home, near a busy road, or running 24/7 on high, check at 6 months. A clogged filter chokes airflow, which collapses the effective CADR — an old filter can make a good purifier perform like a bad one.

Conclusion

Air purifiers are one of the rare products where the honest version of the marketing is still impressive: a correctly sized True HEPA unit genuinely transforms the particle load in a room — allergy sufferers usually feel the difference within days. The failures are almost never the technology; they're the sizing, the placement, or the expectation that one box fixes gases, CO2, and humidity too. Do the ⅔ CADR math, buy bigger than you think, replace the filter on schedule, and let ventilation handle the rest.
