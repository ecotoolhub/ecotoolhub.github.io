Composting Coffee Grounds: C:N Ratio, Mix Guide & Mistakes

Published by Eco Tool Hub Editorial Team • Updated October 2026 • 6 min read

⚡ Quick Answer

Used coffee grounds are a compost "green" with a C:N ratio of about 20:1 — rich in nitrogen. Add them at no more than 20–25% of your pile, always mixed with coarse browns like dry leaves or shredded cardboard. A simple winning mix: 2 parts coffee grounds + 1 part dry leaves ≈ 33:1, right near the 30:1 sweet spot. Brewed grounds are nearly pH-neutral, so the "they make soil acidic" fear is a myth.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • Green, not brown: Coffee grounds are nitrogen-rich (20:1 C:N) — treat them like grass clippings, not like leaves.
  • Acidity is a myth: Used grounds test at pH 6.5–6.8, nearly neutral. The acid went into your cup.
  • The 20% rule: Never let grounds exceed a quarter of the pile — fine particles compact, block air, and turn slimy.
  • Always pair with browns: Dry leaves, cardboard, or straw keep the pile fluffy and the microbes fed.
  • Worms love them: A handful a week in a worm bin is a treat; a bucketful is a disaster.

Are Coffee Grounds a "Green" or a "Brown"?

Color lies. Despite being brown, coffee grounds count as a compost green — the nitrogen side of the pile. Their carbon-to-nitrogen ratio sits around 20:1, close to grass clippings and far from true browns like dry leaves (60:1) or cardboard (350:1). That nitrogen is exactly what heats a pile up: microbes burn carbon for energy and use nitrogen to build their bodies, and a pile starved of nitrogen just sits there, cold and unchanged, for months.

The practical consequence: every scoop of grounds you add needs a matching scoop (or two) of browns. Skip the browns and you get the classic beginner failure — a dense, wet, sour-smelling mat that molds instead of composting.

The Acidity Myth, Debunked

This is the most repeated composting myth on the internet: "coffee grounds are acidic, so they'll acidify your soil." Lab tests tell a different story. Used (brewed) coffee grounds measure pH 6.5–6.8 — essentially neutral — because brewing washes most of the acids into your cup. Finished compost that includes grounds typically lands between pH 6.5 and 7.0, which suits nearly all garden plants.

The one exception: unbrewed grounds (stale beans, espresso pucks from a cafe that were never brewed) are genuinely acidic and much stronger in caffeine. If a coffee shop gives you bags of grounds, ask whether they're spent — spent is what you want.

The Right Mix Ratio (With Numbers)

The target for a hot, fast pile is a C:N ratio near 30:1. Here is how coffee grounds compare with common browns:

Coffee grounds (used)Green20:1
Grass clippingsGreen20:1
Kitchen food scrapsGreen15:1
Dry fall leavesBrown60:1
StrawBrown80:1
Shredded cardboardBrown350:1
Paper coffee filtersBrown400:1

A dead-simple recipe that works: 2 buckets of coffee grounds + 1 bucket of dry leaves ≈ 33:1 — close enough to the 30:1 sweet spot that your pile will heat within days. If you only have cardboard, go thinner on it: half a bucket of shredded cardboard per 2 buckets of grounds lands near 30:1 as well, because cardboard is so carbon-dense.

5 Mistakes to Avoid

  • Dumping grounds in thick layers. A 10-cm slab of pure grounds seals like concrete — water runs off, air can't enter, and the middle goes anaerobic and stinks. Always scatter thin and mix in.
  • Adding grounds with no browns. Grounds + kitchen scraps with nothing carbon-rich is a recipe for a slimy, fruit-fly-covered mess. Every green needs its brown.
  • Piling uncomposted grounds deep around plants. A thin top-dressing (under 1 cm) is fine as slow-release mulch; a thick ring crusts over, sheds water, and can grow mold against stems.
  • Believing caffeine will poison the garden. Caffeine breaks down during composting, and the amounts in spent grounds are tiny. Finished compost with grounds is safe for vegetables, flowers, and lawns.
  • Forgetting the filters. Tossing the grounds but binning the paper filter wastes a perfect brown. Unbleached filters tear up easily and balance the grounds' nitrogen beautifully.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will coffee grounds make my compost or soil too acidic?

No. Used (brewed) coffee grounds test at pH 6.5–6.8, nearly neutral — most of the acid washes into your cup. Finished compost containing grounds typically lands at pH 6.5–7.0, fine for almost all plants.

How much coffee grounds can I add to my compost pile?

Keep grounds at 20–25% of total pile volume. More than that and the fine particles compact, block airflow, and turn the pile slimy and anaerobic. Always mix with coarse browns like dry leaves or shredded cardboard.

Can I put coffee grounds directly on my plants without composting?

Yes, as a thin top-dressing (under 1 cm) around established plants — it acts as a slow-release nitrogen mulch. Thick layers crust over, repel water, and can mold, so never pile it on deep.

Do worms like coffee grounds in a worm bin?

Worms love them in moderation — about a handful per week for a standard bin, mixed with bedding. The grit aids their digestion. Too much at once overheats the bin and drives worms away.

Can I compost paper coffee filters too?

Yes — unbleached paper filters are a carbon-rich "brown" (roughly 400:1 C:N) and balance the nitrogen in the grounds perfectly. Tear them up first so they break down faster.

Conclusion

Coffee grounds are one of the best free compost ingredients you can get — nitrogen-rich, worm-approved, and pH-neutral once brewed. The entire game is proportion: keep them under a quarter of the pile, pair every scoop with browns, and aim for that 30:1 C:N ratio. Do that, and your morning coffee keeps working long after the cup is empty.
