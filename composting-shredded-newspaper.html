Composting Shredded Newspaper: Safe Inks & Mix Guide

Published by Eco Tool Hub Editorial Team • Updated October 2026 • 6 min read

⚡ Quick Answer

Newsprint is a mid-strength compost "brown" at about 175:1 C:N, and since the 1990s most newspaper inks are soy- or water-based and compost-safe. Shred it into 2–3 cm strips (a cross-cut shredder is ideal), soak or crumple it, and mix — roughly one shredded Sunday paper balances one bucket of kitchen scraps. Keep glossy ad inserts and magazines out: their coatings do not break down.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • Mid-strength brown: At ~175:1, newspaper sits between leaves (60:1) and cardboard (350:1) — perfect when the leaf bag runs out.
  • Inks are (mostly) fine now: Black-and-white newsprint uses soy/water-based inks. The heavy-metal inks of the 1970s are long gone.
  • Glossy = out: Shiny magazine paper and ad inserts are clay- and plastic-coated — they will survive your pile intact.
  • Strips, not sheets: Flat sheets mat into papier-mâché. Strips of 2–3 cm, or scrunched balls, keep air moving through the pile.
  • The indoor brown: Newspaper stores dry, clean and pest-free in a cupboard — the only brown available in a January blizzard.

Is Newspaper Ink Safe? The Updated Answer

The caution around newspaper ink is a fossil from the 1970s, when some inks carried petroleum solvents and trace heavy-metal pigments. Modern newsprint is printed overwhelmingly with soy- or water-based inks whose pigments are carbon black and mineral colorants at low concentrations. Newspaper is also one of the most-tested compost inputs in existence — universities have run worm bins and compost trials on shredded newsprint for decades without contamination findings.

The part of the paper bundle that still deserves your suspicion is not the ink — it is the gloss. Magazine pages and shiny inserts are coated with clay and plastic films that do not biodegrade; they emerge from the pile as intact, papery ghosts. Sort those out for recycling and compost the honest newsprint.

Shred Size and the Papier-Mâché Problem

Newspaper has a failure mode all its own: add whole sheets to a moist pile and pressure plus water weld them into a single laminated slab — papier-mâché that sheds water, blocks air, and can survive a year. The prevention is mechanical: shred to 2–3 cm strips (a cross-cut office shredder is perfect), or tear into strips and scrunch each handful into a loose ball before adding.

Wetted strips behave beautifully — absorbent, moldable, quick to rot — and shredded newspaper doubles as the classic worm-bin bedding. Its strategic value is timing: leaves vanish by spring, but newspaper is generated every week of the year and stores indefinitely in a dry cupboard, ready to balance the first flush of grass clippings.

Mixing Shredded Newspaper: Ratios

Newspaper at 175:1 is a genuine brown but an unusually light one — dose by handfuls:

Shredded newspaperBrown175:1
Kitchen food scrapsGreen15:1
Grass clippingsGreen20:1
Coffee groundsGreen20:1
Dry fall leavesBrown60:1
Shredded cardboardBrown350:1

By the weighted math, 1 part shredded newspaper + 2 parts grass clippings ≈ 72:1 still reads carbon-heavy — but newsprint is so light per handful that the working dose is gentler than the ratio implies: a generous double-handful of damp strips per bucket of scraps, added gradually until the pile smells clean. When several browns are available, blend them — newspaper plus leaves beats either one alone.

5 Newspaper Mistakes to Avoid

  • Composting the glossy inserts. The ads bundle is the trap: shiny paper, coupon books and magazine sections are coated and will not rot. Newsprint only.
  • Adding whole folded sections. A folded broadsheet is a prefabricated papier-mâché slab. Shred or scrunch every piece.
  • Using newspaper as the only brown for a wet pile. Re-wetted strips can re-mat under pressure. Partner them with a chunky brown (cardboard, straw) for structure.
  • Shredding window envelopes with the windows in. Those clear panels are plastic film. Rip windows out before the envelope joins the shredder pile.
  • Assuming the colored funnies are toxic. Modern color inks on newsprint are as safe as the black. It is gloss, not color, that disqualifies paper.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are soy-based newspaper inks really safe for vegetable gardens?

Yes. Modern newsprint inks are soy- or water-based, and decades of research use — including university worm-bin studies built on shredded newspaper — show no contamination concern. The part to exclude is glossy paper, not the ink.

How long does shredded newspaper take to compost?

In an active pile, 2-3 cm strips disappear in 6-10 weeks. In a worm bin, damp shredded newspaper is eaten through in 3-6 weeks. Whole sheets, by contrast, can take a year.

Can shredded newspaper replace cardboard in a worm bin?

Yes — it is the traditional bedding. Use strips, dampen them to a wrung-out sponge consistency, and fluff weekly. Many keepers mix newspaper with some shredded cardboard for added structure.

Newspaper or leaves — which brown is better?

Leaves win on biology (they arrive carrying fungi and microbes) and on structure. Newspaper wins on availability and storage: dry, pest-free and on hand in January. A mix of both is better than either alone.

Can I compost the whole newspaper including the ad inserts?

Only the newsprint sections. Pull out glossy inserts, shiny coupon booklets and any plastic wrapping — those coatings do not biodegrade and will fleck your finished compost with undecomposed pieces.

Conclusion

Shredded newspaper is the brown of last resort that turns out to be a brown of first convenience: safe inks, year-round supply, cupboard storage. Shred it fine, wet it, keep the glossy stuff out, and the Sunday paper keeps working all week. Balance your greens against it with the compost calculator above.
