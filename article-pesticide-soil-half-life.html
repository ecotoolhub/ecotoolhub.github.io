How Long Do Pesticides Stay in Soil? Half-Life Timelines for 12 Common Chemicals

Published by Eco Tool Hub Editorial Team • October 18, 2026 • 7 min read

🌱 Quick Answer

It depends on the chemical — malathion is ~97% gone in 5 days, while glyphosate (Roundup) needs about 8 months and imidacloprid can persist over a year. The rule environmental scientists use: after 5 half-lives, ~97% has degraded. Typical soil half-lives from university extension data: malathion 1 day, carbaryl 10 days, permethrin 30 days, diazinon 40 days, glyphosate 47 days, atrazine 60 days, imidacloprid ~100 days. Warm, moist, microbe-rich soil breaks everything down faster.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • The 5-half-life rule: after 5 half-lives, ~97% of a pesticide has degraded; after 7, less than 1% remains. That's the practical meaning of "gone."
  • Short-lived ≠ weak: malathion (1 day) and carbaryl (10 days) kill pests effectively, then vanish — which is exactly why gardeners prefer them near harvest.
  • The long tail is real: imidacloprid (~100 days) and legacy DDT (years) keep working — and keep exposing soil life — long after spraying day.
  • Conditions swing the numbers: the same chemical can last 2–3× longer in cold, dry, low-microbe soil than in warm, moist, compost-rich soil.

The 5-Half-Life Rule: How Soil Decay Actually Works

A pesticide's soil half-life is the time it takes for half of the chemical in the soil to break down — eaten by microbes, split by sunlight, or dissolved by water. After one half-life, 50% remains. After two, 25%. The math never quite reaches zero, but it gets close fast:

Watch it with a real example — glyphosate at a 47-day half-life, starting from 100% on spraying day:

This is why extension scientists classify pesticides by persistence: nonpersistent (half-life ≤ 30 days), moderately persistent (31–99 days), and persistent (100+ days). The class tells you at a glance whether you're dealing with a days-long or a seasons-long commitment.

When Is It Safe to Plant Again?

The honest answer has two layers:

  • The legal layer: every pesticide label lists plant-back or re-entry intervals — those are legal requirements, not suggestions. Always follow the label first.
  • The math layer: for planning beyond the label (rotating beds, going organic), apply the 5-half-life rule. Sprayed glyphosate in spring? By ~8 months (~235 days) it's ~97% degraded. Used imidacloprid grub control? Budget 500+ days before calling that soil clean.

For food gardens, many organic growers add a safety margin: wait the label interval plus one extra half-life, and never plant root crops (carrots, potatoes, beets) first in a recently treated bed — roots sit in the residue zone longest. When in doubt, a basic soil test from your local extension office costs little and answers the question for your exact soil.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does Roundup (glyphosate) stay in soil?

Glyphosate has a typical soil half-life of about 47 days (Montana State University Extension). Half is gone in ~7 weeks, and roughly 97% has degraded after five half-lives — about 235 days, or just under 8 months. Actual persistence varies with soil type, temperature, and microbial activity, so always follow the replant interval on the product label.

Which garden pesticides break down fastest in soil?

Among common garden chemicals, malathion breaks down fastest with a soil half-life of about 1 day, followed by the fungicide captan (~2.5 days) and acephate (~3 days). These short-lived products are essentially gone within a week or two under normal conditions.

Do pesticides ever fully disappear from soil?

Effectively, yes — after 5 half-lives about 97% of a chemical has degraded, and after 7 half-lives less than 1% remains. The exception is legacy organochlorines like DDT, which persisted in soil for 2 to 15 years. Modern garden pesticides are designed to break down far faster.

Does composting destroy pesticide residues?

Hot, active composting breaks down short-lived pesticides quickly thanks to heat and intense microbial activity. But persistent chemicals — imidacloprid, DDT, and similar long-half-life compounds — can survive the composting process. Never compost plants treated with persistent pesticides if the compost will go on food crops.

How do I calculate how much pesticide is left in my soil?

Use the half-life formula: remaining = initial amount × (1/2)^(days elapsed ÷ half-life). For example, with glyphosate's 47-day half-life, 94 days after spraying leaves (1/2)^(94/47) = 25% of the original amount. Our free half-life calculator does this math for you instantly.

Conclusion

Every pesticide comes with a built-in expiry date — you just have to read it in half-lives. Malathion's is measured in days, glyphosate's in months, imidacloprid's in seasons. Multiply by five, and you have your practical "cleared" date. Match that date against your planting calendar, keep the label's intervals as your legal floor, and let the calculator above turn any spray date into an exact residue estimate. Your soil is a living system — treat its recovery time as part of the plan, not an afterthought.
