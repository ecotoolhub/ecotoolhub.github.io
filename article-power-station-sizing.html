What Size Portable Power Station Do You Need? A Simple Sizing Guide

Published by Eco Tool Hub Editorial Team • November 24, 2026 • 8 min read

Quick Answer

List what you want to run, multiply each item's watts by the hours you will use it, and add it all up — that is your watt-hours (Wh). Divide by 0.85, because only about 85% of a battery's rated capacity is usable, and buy the next size up. Then check the second number on the box: the inverter's continuous watts must cover your biggest single appliance, plus headroom for motor start-up surges. For most homes that lands at about 500Wh for lights, phones and a fan, 1,500Wh once a fridge joins the list, and 1,000Wh if a CPAP has to run all night.

Key Takeaways

  • Watts × hours = watt-hours. Your shopping list is just addition — no guesswork needed.
  • Only ~85% of rated capacity is usable; the inverter loses the rest as heat. Always divide by 0.85.
  • Capacity (Wh) and inverter size (W) are two different numbers — a big battery with a small inverter still cannot start a fridge.
  • Motors surge: a fridge compressor can briefly draw 2–3× its running watts when it starts.
  • Heating loads (kettle, space heater) empty batteries in minutes — they are the last thing to size for.

Step 1: Understand the two numbers on the box

Every power station advertises two numbers, and confusing them is the most common sizing mistake. Capacity, measured in watt-hours (Wh), is how much energy is in the tank — it decides how long things run. Inverter output, measured in watts (W), is how much power the station can deliver at one moment — it decides what can run at all.

The bridge between them is one line of arithmetic: watts × hours = watt-hours. A 100W television watched for 3 hours uses 300Wh, whether it draws it from a small station or a large one. A 1,000Wh station is simply a tank that holds 1,000 of those units — minus losses, which brings us to the rule that keeps the maths honest.

Step 2: Add up your loads, then apply the 85% rule

Write down each appliance, its watts (printed on a label on the back or in its manual), and the hours per day you honestly need it. Multiply, add the column up, and you have your daily watt-hours. Then divide by 0.85: converting stored DC battery power into household AC power wastes roughly 15% as heat, so a "1,000Wh" station really delivers about 850Wh to your devices. Skipping this step is why so many first stations disappoint on their first real night.

One special case: the fridge. Its compressor cycles on and off, so you do not multiply its watts by 24. A typical modern fridge drawing about 150W while running gets through roughly 900–1,200Wh across a full day, depending on its size, the room temperature and how often the door opens. Keeping the door shut — the same rule as our first-night blackout guide — is worth real watt-hours.

Step 3: Check the inverter — and the fridge surge

Capacity gets the headlines, but the inverter decides whether your biggest appliance runs at all. The station's continuous watts must be higher than the largest single load you will plug in — and for anything with a motor, there is a catch. When a fridge compressor starts, it briefly pulls roughly two to three times its running watts before settling down. A 150W fridge can demand 300–450W for that first moment, and a station whose surge rating cannot cover it will simply shut off, even with a full battery.

The practical rule: pick an inverter rated at least 25% above your biggest load, and if a fridge is on your list, treat 600W continuous as the sensible floor and check the maker's stated surge (peak) watts against motor loads. This is also why kettles and space heaters are poor outage loads: at around 1,500W each they need a large inverter, and they burn through roughly 1,500Wh for every hour of heat — a 1,000Wh station gives you about half an hour. Warmth from blankets and one shared room costs zero watt-hours.

Recharging: wall, car and solar

A station is only as good as its next charge. Wall charging at home is fastest and is how you top up before a forecast storm. In a longer outage, solar is the refill that never runs out: as a planning figure, a 200W panel in about 4 hours of good sun delivers roughly 600Wh after real-world losses — panels rarely produce their full rated watts outside a lab, and angle, heat and cloud all take their share. That is enough to cover lights, phones and WiFi day after day. Two checks before buying panels: the station's maximum solar input (a panel bigger than the limit just wastes money), and whether it can charge and power devices at the same time, which most modern units can.

Safety: the one rule that matters most

A battery power station makes no exhaust, so it is safe to use indoors — keep it dry, give it air while it charges, and stay within its rated load. A fuel generator is the opposite case, and official guidance is blunt about it: Ready.gov and the CDC say generators must only ever run outdoors, at least 20 feet from the home, with the exhaust pointed away from windows and doors. Carbon monoxide from a generator is colourless and odourless and can kill in minutes; a generator in a garage, porch or near an open window is still an indoor generator. If you use one, pair it with a working battery-powered CO alarm — and never refuel it hot or indoors.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size power station do I need to run a fridge?

A modern fridge draws about 150W while its compressor runs and cycles on and off, using roughly 900–1,200Wh over a day. Add lights and phones and you need about 1,500Wh of nameplate capacity for a full day, once you apply the 85% usable rule. The inverter also matters: choose at least 600W continuous with a surge rating that can handle the compressor's start-up spike.

How long will a 1000Wh power station run my appliances?

About 850Wh of a 1000Wh station is usable. That runs LED lights, a WiFi router and phone charging for well over a day, a fridge for most of a day, or a CPAP machine (about 60W without a heated humidifier) for roughly 14 hours. Divide 850 by the appliance's watts to get a rough number of hours.

Can a portable power station run a space heater or electric kettle?

Technically yes if the inverter is rated for it — both typically draw 1,500W — but they drain a battery very fast: a 1000Wh station runs a 1,500W heater for only about 30–35 minutes. Heating with electricity is the least efficient use of stored power; blankets, layers and one shared warm room go much further in an outage.

Can I recharge a power station with solar panels during an outage?

Yes. Most stations accept solar input, and many can charge and power devices at the same time. As a rule of thumb, a 200W panel in about 4 hours of good sun delivers roughly 600Wh after real-world losses — enough to cover lights, phones and WiFi each day. Check the station's maximum solar input watts before buying panels.

Are portable power stations safe to use indoors?

Yes — a battery power station produces no exhaust, so unlike a fuel generator it can be used inside. Ready.gov and the CDC are clear that generators must only ever run outdoors, at least 20 feet from the home with exhaust pointed away, because carbon monoxide from a generator can kill in minutes. For a power station, just keep it dry, ventilated while charging, and within its rated load.

The calm summary

Sizing a power station is three small steps: add up watts × hours for what you truly need, divide by 0.85, and check the inverter against your biggest load and its surge. Do that on paper — or in thirty seconds with our Blackout Power Calculator — and the size on the box stops being a guess. Buy for the fridge, the lights and the phone charger, not for the kettle, and a modest station will quietly carry your home through most outages.
