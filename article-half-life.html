Understanding Radioactive Decay and Geiger Counter Basics

Published by Eco Tool Hub Editorial Team • Updated September 2026 • 10 min read

⚡ Quick Answer

Radioactive half-life is the time required for half of the unstable atomic nuclei in a sample to undergo radioactive decay. Calculated using standard exponential kinetics equations, this decay rate is measured experimentally using handheld Geiger-Muller counters and high-precision 0.001g laboratory balance scales.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • Exponential Kinetics: Nuclear decay rate is constant and unaffected by external temperature or pressure.
  • Radiation Types: Geiger counters detect ionizing Beta particles, Gamma rays, and X-rays in microsieverts per hour (uSv/h).
  • Lab Measurement: Precision digital scales measuring down to 1 milligram (0.001g) are essential for tracking physical sample mass changes.
  • Decay Calculator: Compute remaining radioactive mass and elapsed cycles with our Half-Life Calculator.

1. The Mathematics of Radioactive Decay

Radioactive decay follows first-order exponential kinetics. Regardless of initial quantity, exactly 50% of the remaining parent isotope transforms into daughter products during each half-life interval.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does a Geiger Counter detect ionizing radiation?

Geiger counters contain a gas-filled tube (Geiger-Muller tube). When ionizing radiation passes through the tube, it ionizes the gas, creating an electrical pulse that registers on the digital screen in uSv/h.

What is a safe background radiation reading?

Normal ambient background radiation typically ranges between 0.08 uSv/h and 0.25 uSv/h. Readings consistently above 0.50 uSv/h indicate elevated environmental radiation.
