Blackout: What To Do in the First 4 Hours and the First Night

Published by Eco Tool Hub Editorial Team • November 20, 2026 • 8 min read

Quick Answer

When the power goes out: keep the fridge and freezer doors shut (food stays safe about 4 hours in the fridge, 48 in a full freezer), switch off or unplug sensitive electronics, light the home with battery lanterns — never candles near children or curtains — and save your phone battery for updates. Check on elderly neighbours, turn on a battery radio for official news, and eat perishable food first. Most outages end within hours; a calm first night is mostly about food safety, light, warmth and information.

Key Takeaways

  • The fridge clock starts immediately: 4 hours for the fridge, 48 for a full freezer (USDA). Doors stay shut.
  • Unplug TVs, computers and chargers so the returning surge cannot damage them.
  • Light with LEDs, not flames. Candles are a leading cause of outage fires.
  • A battery or hand-crank radio is your internet when the internet is gone.
  • Plan the night around one warm meal, one charged phone, and everyone sleeping in the warmest room.

The first 15 minutes: protect food and electronics

The lights die, the fridge hum stops, and the house goes quiet. Your first job is not light — it is time. A closed fridge holds safe temperatures for about 4 hours; a full freezer for about 48 hours (24 if half full). Every door opening spends that time, so decide now: nobody opens the fridge "just to look". Put a note on the door if you have children.

Next, walk the house and switch off or unplug anything with a motor or a screen: TVs, computers, games consoles, chargers, the microwave. When power returns it often arrives with a surge, and the cheapest surge protection is an unplugged cable. Leave one small lamp switched on so you know when power is back.

Check your breaker panel once — sometimes the "blackout" is only your own tripped switch. If the whole street is dark, it is the grid, and you settle in.

Hours 1–4: light, news and the perishables plan

Set up light where life happens: one lantern in the kitchen, one in the bathroom, a torch per bedroom. Battery LED light is brighter and safer than candles in every way that matters, especially with children and pets moving around in the dark.

Turn on a battery or hand-crank radio and find the local news station. Official updates tell you the two things you actually need: how wide the outage is, and roughly how long it will last. Put phones into battery-saver mode, drop screen brightness, and agree as a family: one phone stays on for news and calls, the rest go off or into airplane mode. A charged power bank is tomorrow's lifeline, not tonight's entertainment.

Then eat in the right order. Tonight's meal should come from the fridge — milk, leftovers, soft cheese, anything that will not survive tomorrow. Freezer food is tomorrow's problem only if the outage runs long, and the canned and dry cupboard is your reserve. If food in the fridge has been above fridge temperature for more than 4 hours, the USDA rule is blunt: when in doubt, throw it out.

The first night: warmth, sleep and calm children

Move everyone into one room for the evening — body heat is real heating. Close curtains and doors to hold warmth in, dress in layers, and put blankets on beds before bedtime rather than after people are cold. If you have a baby, they sleep where you can see and reach them, dressed one layer warmer than you, with feeds ready: formula made with water from your stored supply, or a ready-to-feed bottle for the first night so nobody is measuring powder by torchlight.

Never bring a charcoal grill, camping stove or generator indoors, and never heat the home with a gas oven. Carbon monoxide is odourless, and it is the quiet killer of every long outage. Cook outside or not at all; a cold meal for one night harms nobody.

Before bed: lock up as usual (alarms may be off, so be deliberate), leave one battery light in the hallway, put torches by each bed, and check once more on any neighbour who lives alone. Then sleep. Most outages are over by morning, and a family that has eaten, stayed warm and slept is ready for day two if it comes.

If it stretches into day two

Day two is a logistics day. Start the supplies maths properly: water at 1 gallon per person per day (US) or 3 litres drinking water per person (UK/EU), food from the freezer first as it thaws, and a written list of what you are short of before any shop trip. Frequently Asked Questions

How long is fridge food safe without power?

About 4 hours if the door stays closed (USDA). A full freezer holds about 48 hours, a half-full one about 24. After that, perishable food should be thrown away — you cannot see or smell most of what makes it unsafe.

Can I use candles during a blackout?

You can, but battery LED lanterns are safer and brighter. Candles near curtains, bedding, children or pets cause fires every major outage. If you do use them, keep them in sturdy holders, on stable surfaces, away from anything flammable, and never leave them burning while you sleep.

Should I unplug everything?

Unplug or switch off sensitive electronics and anything with a motor (TVs, computers, chargers, washing machine). Leave one small lamp on to signal the power's return. Fridges and freezers stay plugged in — just keep their doors shut.

How do I get news if the internet and TV are down?

A battery-powered or hand-crank radio tuned to a local news station. This is why Ready.gov, the UK Be Ready campaign and EU guidance all list a radio in the basic kit — it works when every network is down.

The calm summary

A blackout is an inconvenience with a clock attached. Beat the clock on food (doors shut, eat perishables first), protect your electronics from the surge, light the house with LEDs, get your news from a radio, and sleep warm in one room.
