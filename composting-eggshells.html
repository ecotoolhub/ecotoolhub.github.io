Composting Eggshells: Calcium & Prep Guide

Published by Eco Tool Hub Editorial Team • Updated October 2026 • 6 min read

⚡ Quick Answer

Eggshells are neither a compost "green" nor a "brown" — they are a mineral additive, roughly 95% calcium carbonate with virtually no carbon or nitrogen, so they have no meaningful C:N ratio. Rinse, dry and grind them to powder before adding: whole shells can survive a year or more in a pile almost unchanged, while powder disappears in weeks. A household using 1–2 dozen eggs a week is adding exactly the right amount — there is no pile-balancing math to do, just prep.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • Not a green or brown: Eggshells are ~95% calcium carbonate — a mineral supplement for your pile, not a fuel.
  • Grind or don't bother: Powdered shells break down in weeks; recognizable half-shells can sit intact for over a year.
  • Calcium for blossom end rot: Composted shells slowly feed the calcium that helps tomatoes and peppers resist blossom end rot.
  • Rinse first: Egg residue left on shells attracts flies and smells — a quick rinse and dry fixes it completely.
  • No pH panic: Shells buffer mildly against acidity but will not meaningfully change a healthy pile's pH.

Why Eggshells Don't Have a C:N Ratio

Every compost recipe is a fuel calculation: carbon for energy, nitrogen for protein. Eggshells supply neither in any useful amount. A shell is a mineral shell — calcium carbonate with a thin protein membrane — closer to a pebble than to a leaf. Microbes do not eat it for fuel; instead, the mild acids produced inside an active pile dissolve it slowly, releasing calcium into the finished compost.

That is why you will never see eggshells in a C:N chart with a real number, and why adding more shells never heats a pile up or slows one down. They ride along with whatever greens and browns you already balanced, and their contribution shows up later, in the soil, as plant-available calcium.

Prep Matters More Than Quantity: Rinse, Dry, Grind

Rinse each shell under the tap as you crack it — leftover egg white is what attracts flies and causes smell complaints. Dry them (a tray on the counter for a day, or 10 minutes in a 100°C oven as it cools down) so they turn brittle. Then crush: a mortar and pestle, the bottom of a jar, a rolling pin over a tea towel, or a quick blender blitz for a week's worth at once.

Surface area is the whole story. Powdered shell exposes thousands of times more mineral to pile acids than a half-shell does, so it dissolves in 1–3 months instead of lingering past a year. If you keep finding white shell shards in your finished compost, your grind is too coarse — not your pile too slow.

How Many Eggshells Should You Add?

There is no ratio to solve for shells themselves — but they still belong in the pile picture, because everything around them follows the usual rules:

Coffee groundsGreen20:1

StrawBrown80:1

Dose by rhythm, not by math: a small handful of shell powder per bucket of greens keeps calcium trickling in without any risk of excess. There is no realistic overdose from a home kitchen — you would need kilograms of shells a week to matter. Gardeners who bake in winter often save a jar of powder and work it into spring piles, where it buffers piles built from acidic inputs like pine needles or heavy coffee grounds.

5 Eggshell Mistakes to Avoid

  • Tossing in whole or half shells. They become archaeology: unchanged white cups that you sieve out of finished compost season after season. Grind first, always.
  • Skipping the rinse. Dried egg white on shells is a fly magnet and the real source of "eggshells stink" stories. Ten seconds under the tap solves it.
  • Expecting instant calcium. Shell calcium is slow-release. This season's shells feed next season's beds — for a blossom-end-rot rescue right now, fix watering consistency first and use a foliar calcium spray.
  • Using shells as a slug barrier. The sharp-shell slug deterrent is a durable myth; controlled tests show slugs stroll over crushed shell unbothered. Compost them for calcium instead.
  • Blending wet shells. Wet shells smear into a smelly paste that coats the blender. Dry shells blitz cleanly into powder in seconds.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do eggshells need to be baked before composting?

No, baking is optional. It makes shells more brittle and easier to crush, and it kills any salmonella on raw shells, but plain air-dried shells compost perfectly well. If you do bake, 10 minutes at 100°C (210°F) is plenty.

How long do eggshells take to break down in compost?

Ground to powder, they largely dissolve into the compost in 1-3 months in an active pile. Coarsely crushed pieces can take 6-12 months, and small shell fragments in finished compost are harmless — they keep releasing calcium slowly in the soil.

Can eggshells raise my compost or soil pH?

Only very slightly. Calcium carbonate is a mild buffer, and the amount from a home kitchen is tiny compared with the volume of a pile. Shells will not fix genuinely acidic soil — use garden lime for that job.

Do eggshells attract rats or other pests to the pile?

Unrinsed shells with egg-white residue can. Rinsed, dried shells are inert mineral and attract nothing. As with all kitchen inputs, mix shell powder into the middle of the pile rather than leaving it on top.

Are brown eggshells better than white eggshells for compost?

They are nutritionally identical. Shell color comes from the breed of hen, not from calcium content. Use whatever comes out of your kitchen.

Conclusion

Eggshells are the one compost input with no math attached: rinse them, dry them, grind them fine, and let pile acids do the rest. You get slow, steady calcium for the garden and one less thing in the trash.
