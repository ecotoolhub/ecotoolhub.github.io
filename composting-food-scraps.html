Composting Food Scraps: Burying & Mix Guide

Published by Eco Tool Hub Editorial Team • Updated October 2026 • 6 min read

⚡ Quick Answer

Kitchen food scraps are a nitrogen-rich "green" at about 15:1 C:N — wet, dense, and the input most likely to make a pile smell and breed flies. The fix is depth: bury every addition 20–25 cm into the pile's center and cap it with browns. The balance to aim for is 3 parts scraps + 2 parts dry leaves ≈ 33:1. And keep the short no-list out of an open pile: no meat, fish, dairy, oils or greasy cooked food.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • Strongest kitchen green: At 15:1, scraps heat a pile fast — and overwhelm it just as fast without browns on hand.
  • Bury 25 cm, always: Surface scraps are an open invitation to flies, wasps, rats and raccoons. Depth is the whole defense.
  • The no-list is short but firm: No meat, fish, dairy, oils or saucy cooked food in an open pile — those need sealed systems.
  • Freeze first, fuss less: A tub of scraps kept in the freezer kills fruit-fly eggs, stops counter smells, and makes chopping easy.
  • Chop big, rot fast: A melon rind in chunks vanishes in weeks; left whole, it can outlast the summer.

A Green That Punches Above Its Weight (15:1)

Food scraps are 70–85% water wrapped around concentrated nitrogen and sugars — closer to a slurry than a solid. In a pile they behave accordingly: they collapse into dense, airless pockets where the aerobic workforce suffocates, and fermentation takes over. Nearly every "my compost stinks and has flies" story is, at bottom, a story about scraps added faster than browns and left too near the surface.

Managed well, the same scraps are the engine of a home pile: the fastest common route to real heat, and the ingredient that lets a small household compost meaningfully at all. The difference between engine and problem is never the scraps themselves — it is depth, chopping, and the brown cap.

The Bury-and-Cap Method

Every trip to the pile goes like this: fork open a hole in the pile's center, tip the scraps in, mix them with the surrounding material rather than leaving a pure layer, then cap with 10 cm of browns — leaves, shredded cardboard, straw. Buried scraps heat fast, smell of nothing, and are physically out of reach of flies and rodents. Surface scraps do the opposite on all three counts.

Two supporting habits: keep a lidded caddy indoors and empty it every day or two (or store scraps in the freezer, which also kills the fruit-fly eggs already present in banana skins and fruit rinds), and chop anything bigger than a fist. For the no-list foods, the sealed routes — bokashi buckets, electric composters, municipal green waste — handle them properly; an open pile is simply the wrong tool.

Balancing Food Scraps: The Ratio

Scraps at 15:1 need roughly their own volume again in browns. The full picture:

MaterialTypeApprox. C:N
Kitchen food scrapsGreen15:1
Dry fall leavesBrown60:1
Shredded cardboardBrown350:1
StrawBrown80:1
Shredded newspaperBrown175:1
Coffee groundsGreen20:1
Grass clippingsGreen20:1

3 parts scraps + 2 parts dry leaves ≈ 33:1 is the household standard. The habit that makes it effortless: keep a "brown bucket" beside the kitchen caddy — shredded cardboard, torn newspaper, a scoop of stored leaves — so every scraps trip carries its own cover with it. If the pile ever smells sweet-sour on approach, the ratio has slipped: add browns, not apologies.

5 Food Scrap Mistakes to Avoid

  • Leaving the countertop caddy open for days. That is not a caddy; it is a fruit-fly nursery with a handle. Empty it every 1–2 days or keep scraps frozen.
  • Pouring in cooking oil. Oil coats material, blocks air and water, and slows everything it touches. A trace of oily dressing is fine; a pan of fryer oil is a pile-killer.
  • Adding whole corn cobs, pumpkins and melon shells. Big intact items rot from the outside at a glacial pace. Chop to fist-size or smaller.
  • Expecting an open pile to handle meat safely. Even a hot pile struggles with the pest attention meat attracts. Sealed systems exist precisely for this — use them.
  • Blaming scraps for a cold winter pile. In deep winter the pile's biology is dormant, not broken. Bury scraps deeper, insulate the bin, or bank frozen scraps until spring activity returns.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my compost smell bad after I add food scraps?

Surface-level or over-concentrated scraps go anaerobic and ferment. Bury scraps 20-25 cm into the pile, mix browns in as you add them, and cap with about 10 cm of leaves or cardboard. A balanced pile smells like forest floor, not garbage.

Can I compost meat, cheese and cooked leftovers?

Not in an open pile: they attract rats and flies and can carry pathogens. Use a sealed route instead — bokashi fermentation, an electric composter, or a municipal green-waste program. A verified hot pile can process small amounts buried deep, but pest pressure remains the bigger problem.

How do I keep fruit flies away from my compost?

Freeze scraps before adding them (this kills eggs already in the fruit), bury everything, and always cap with browns. A thick brown lid on the pile breaks the flies' breeding cycle within about a week.

Should food scraps go in a worm bin instead?

Fruit and vegetable scraps, yes — worms excel at them. Keep citrus, onion and spicy foods modest in worm bins, and never add meat or dairy. A worm bin for produce plus an outdoor pile for volume is the classic two-system setup.

How long do food scraps take to compost?

Soft scraps vanish in 2-6 weeks in an active pile. The tough items — corn cobs, avocado pits, thick rinds — take 2-6 months unless they are chopped small first.

Conclusion

Food scraps are not the problem child of composting — surface food scraps are. Bury them deep, cap them brown, keep the no-list for sealed systems, and your kitchen waste becomes the heat that drives the whole pile.
