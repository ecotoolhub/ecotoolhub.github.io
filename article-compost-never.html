What NOT to Compost: 15 Things That Ruin Your Pile (and What to Do Instead)

Published by Eco Tool Hub Editorial Team • November 12, 2026 • 7 min read

🌱 Quick Answer

Keep animal products, diseased plants, and anything chemically treated out of your pile. Meat, dairy, fats, and cooked food attract rats and smell awful; diseased leaves and weeds survive a home pile and come back to infect your garden; treated wood, glossy paper, and charcoal ash can leave chemicals in your soil. The simple rule: if it came from an animal, was sprayed or treated, or was ever diseased — it does not go in. Everything else on the list below has a better home, and we tell you where each one goes instead.

💡 Key Takeaways

  • Never compost meat, bones, dairy, or cooked food — pests, smell, and pathogens a home pile cannot kill.
  • Diseased plants and weed seeds survive home composting — bag them for municipal green waste instead.
  • Treated wood, glossy paper, and charcoal ash can leave chemicals and heavy metals in finished compost.
  • Pet waste is not manure — dog and cat waste stays out of any pile that feeds food plants.
  • The "sometimes" list is real: citrus, onions, coffee grounds, and eggshells are fine in moderation — the table below shows the limits.

Why "Everything Natural" Is the Wrong Rule

Compost feels like it should accept anything that once lived. It does not. A working pile is a living system of microbes, and it only handles what those microbes can break down safely at the temperatures a home pile actually reaches. A backyard pile usually runs warm, not hot — it rarely holds the sustained high heat that kills pathogens, weed seeds, and plant diseases. That is the whole story behind this list: the items below are not "bad" in themselves, they are simply things a home pile cannot process safely. Municipal composting plants run far hotter and can take some of them, which is why the "do this instead" column matters as much as the "no" column.

The second test is what the finished compost touches. Compost goes on food plants and into soil your family grows in, so anything that leaves a residue — pesticide, preservative, plastic coating, or heavy metal — ends up in that soil. Plain kitchen scraps, leaves, grass, and uncoated cardboard pass both tests easily. The 15 items below fail one or both. The Never List: 15 Things to Keep Out

These are the hard no's for any home pile. Each one has a real reason — pests, disease, chemicals, or a smell your neighbours will notice — and a better destination.

The pattern is worth memorising: animal products bring pests, diseased material brings disease back, and treated material brings chemicals in. If an item fails any one of those three tests, it stays out. For the timing side — how long clean material actually takes to become compost — see our compost timeline by method.

The "Sometimes" List: Fine in Moderation

These five get blamed unfairly, but they are safe in small amounts — the dose is what matters. Citrus peels and onion scraps are fine scattered through a pile, but a whole bag of oranges at once makes the pile too acidic for the microbes and slows everything down. Coffee grounds are a great nitrogen source, yet a thick layer of them mats together, goes airless, and turns slimy — mix them in, never dump them in a slab. Eggshells are welcome, but they break down slowly unless you crush them first; whole shells can sit in finished compost unchanged for a year. Wood ash is genuinely useful in small amounts (a handful per layer adds minerals), but a bucket of it makes the pile too alkaline — and it must be plain wood ash, never charcoal ash. And plain shredded newspaper is a perfect brown, as long as it is black-and-white newsprint, not the glossy colour inserts.

The practical rule for all five: no single item should ever dominate a layer. Mix it in with other material, keep each addition small, and the pile handles it without complaint. The one exception is a very small pile — if your bin is tiny, even moderate amounts of citrus or coffee can tip the balance, so go lighter still.

5 Mistakes That Quietly Ruin a Good Pile

  • 1. Using the pile as a bin. The moment "just this once" becomes a habit — a plate of leftovers here, a greasy pizza box there — the pile turns into a pest buffet. Keep a small countertop caddy for kitchen scraps and only what belongs in the pile goes in it.
  • 2. Burying problems instead of removing them. Tossing a diseased tomato plant in the middle of the pile and covering it with leaves does not fix it — the disease is still there when you spread the compost. Bag diseased material the day you spot it.
  • 3. Adding whole eggshells and banana peels. They do break down eventually, but whole, they sit for months. Crush shells, chop peels, and everything works faster — see our banana peel guide for the details.
  • 4. Trusting "biodegradable" labels. Many "compostable" bags and cups only break down in industrial facilities at high heat. In a home pile they shred into plastic-looking flakes. If it is not certified for home composting, treat it as trash.
  • 5. Ignoring the smell. A healthy pile smells like forest soil. A rotten-egg or ammonia smell means something wrong went in — usually too much wet food, grease, or a layer gone airless. The fix is the same as the prevention: turn the pile, add dry browns like shredded cardboard or dry leaves, and stop adding the problem item.

When to Break the Rules

Every rule has an exception, and this list has two. A bokashi system ferments food waste — including meat, dairy, and cooked food — in a sealed bucket before it goes into soil, so it handles exactly the items a normal pile cannot. If your household produces a lot of those, bokashi is the upgrade that keeps them out of landfill. A hot, well-managed pile is the second exception: a large pile that genuinely holds high heat for days can kill weed seeds and some pathogens — but be honest about whether yours does. Most home piles never get there, and guessing wrong means weeds and disease across the whole garden next year. When in doubt, the safe route is also the easy one: keep it out and let the municipal service handle it. Frequently Asked Questions

Can I compost meat and dairy?

Not in a regular home compost pile. Meat, bones, dairy, and cooked food attract rats and flies, smell terrible as they rot, and can carry pathogens that a home pile never gets hot enough to kill. They belong in a municipal green bin, a bokashi system, or a commercial composting service — never in a backyard pile.

Can I compost diseased plants?

No. Most home piles never sustain the high temperatures needed to kill plant diseases and weed seeds, so diseased leaves and stems come back with the finished compost and infect next season's plants. Bag diseased material and put it in the trash or a municipal green-waste service, which composts at much higher temperatures.

Can I compost glossy paper and cardboard?

Plain uncoated cardboard and newspaper are fine, but glossy, waxed, or plastic-coated paper is not — the coatings do not break down and can leave plastic and ink residues in your soil. The same goes for receipts, which are usually printed on thermal paper. Tear up plain brown cardboard instead.

Can I compost pet waste?

No, not in a pile that will feed food plants. Dog and cat waste can carry parasites and pathogens that survive home composting. It should go in the trash or a dedicated pet-waste composter kept far from vegetable beds. Manure from herbivores like horses, cows, and chickens is different — it is safe once aged, and our manure guides cover each type.

What is the biggest composting mistake beginners make?

Treating the pile like a trash can. The rule that saves most piles is simple: if it came from an animal, was treated with chemicals, or was ever diseased, keep it out. A pile built from clean kitchen scraps, leaves, and plain cardboard turns into rich compost; a pile with meat, grease, and treated wood turns into a smelly pest magnet.

Conclusion

Good compost is mostly about what you leave out. Keep animal products, diseased material, and anything chemically treated away from the pile, and the ordinary stuff — vegetable scraps, leaves, grass, and plain cardboard — does the rest on its own. Print the table above or keep it on your phone, and the next time you are standing over the bin wondering about something, run the three tests: animal, treated, diseased.
